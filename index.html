Asking Better Questions

Every investigation starts with a question, and a bad question can pick the villain before you’ve looked at a single source.

Content How does the way we ask a question shape what we find? Skills Formulating research questions (Criterion B) Session Lesson 4: Question sort → Can you research it? → Your turn

01Three Kinds of Question

This week you’ve met three cases: The Lorax, the Aral Sea and Easter Island. You’ve also written your own questions in the 3, 2, 1 Bridge. Not all questions do the same job, and an investigation needs to know which kind it’s asking.

Factual

The answer can be looked up and checked. There is one right answer.

“How much did the Aral Sea shrink?”

Conceptual

It explains a pattern that shows up in more than one case.

“Why do shared resources get overused?”

Debatable

Reasonable people disagree, and they argue using evidence.

“Who was most to blame for Rapa Nui’s lost forest?”

You’ve seen these before

Every MYP unit is built on one factual, one conceptual and one debatable question. This unit asks: What role do humans have in environmental destruction? · What role does equity play in conservation and resource management? · Can technological innovation alone solve the environmental challenges we face? Can you tell which is which?

03Can You Actually Research It?

A good research question isn’t just interesting. It has to be answerable, with sources you can actually find. Test every question against these four checks.

1
One issue. Not “the environment”: one resource, one biome, one problem.
2
A clear place or scale. Where? Individual, local or global?
3
It doesn’t assume its answer. If the question already names the villain, it isn’t an investigation.
4
It can be answered with 4–6 good sources. Too big and you drown; too small and there’s nothing to find.
A loaded question is a narrative in disguise.

On Thursday you told Easter Island’s story from inside one narrative, with one villain. That’s fine for a story. But if your research question picks the villain, every source you find will look like proof. Research questions stay open, and the evidence decides.

Weak → stronger: tap to fix

Decide what’s wrong with each weak question before you reveal a stronger version.

Weak“Why did the islanders destroy their island?”
Stronger“How far do rats, rather than people, explain the loss of Rapa Nui’s palm forest?”

Fails check 3: it blames the islanders before looking at the evidence. The stronger version names two explanations and lets the evidence weigh them.

Weak“What happened to the Aral Sea?”
Stronger“How has the shrinking of the Aral Sea affected the health of people living near its former shoreline since 1990?”

Fails checks 1 and 4: it asks for the whole story, so there’s no focus. The stronger version picks one impact, one group of people and a timeframe.

Weak“Is deforestation bad?”
Stronger“Who has benefited most, and who has lost most, from palm oil expansion in Borneo since 2000?”

Fails checks 1, 2 and 3: no place, no focus, and the answer is assumed. The stronger version is still debatable, but it points you towards evidence about real people.

04Your Research Question Toolkit

Come back to this section next week, when you choose your own issue and write your first research question.

Toolkit The funnel: from topic to question ▼

Most weak questions are just topics with a question mark. Narrow down one step at a time:

TopicCoral reefs
IssueCoral bleaching
PlaceSouthern Thailand
AngleThe role of tourism
TimeSince 2000

→ “To what extent has tourism contributed to coral reef decline in southern Thailand since 2000?”

Toolkit Question starters: which work, which don’t ▼
Try theseBe careful with these
To what extent… (weighs a cause)Why is X destroying Y… (assumes the answer)
How has… changed / affected…Is X good / bad? (too broad, opinion only)
Who has benefited, and who has lost…What is…? (usually factual; there’s nothing to investigate)
How far does… explain…What if…? (interesting, but hard to research with sources)
Toolkit Why does it matter? Justifying your question ▼

A research question also needs a reason to exist. Use the lens you know from the 3Ys:

  • Individual: why does this matter to people’s daily lives, health or choices?
  • Local: why does this matter to a community or a place?
  • Global: why does this matter beyond one country?

Justify means giving a reason and evidence, such as one fact from a source, not just “because it’s important.”

05Your Turn

Lesson 4 Write, test, improve (on paper) ▼

Write these on paper. The thinking is the point, so keep it in your own handwriting.
